# RESEARCH — keeping young children engaged in educational games

Findings from six web-research agents (2026-11-14), written to be reused in any session or project.
Sections 1–5 are general (ages 4–8). Section 6 applies them to Hazelnut Hollow.

## How far to trust this

- The agents mostly read **search-result summaries, not full papers**. Effect sizes and sample details are unverified; check the original before quoting a number.
- Most underlying studies are on **older children or adults**. Direct evidence at age 6 is thin.
- Evidence labels: **strong** (meta-analysis or review), **moderate** (a few studies, or the wrong age group), **weak** (one study, inference, or product practice only).
- Nothing was found on ideal session length, platformer difficulty for young children, or the right fun-to-learning ratio. Numbers given for those are practitioner guesses.

## 1. Ranked techniques

Ranked by how many independent research angles converged on the technique and how good the evidence is.

| # | Technique | In practice | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Make the learning the game's verb** | The skill is what you do to play: trace a letter to build the bridge, count acorns to open the gate. No quiz gate between the fun. | Moderate. Habgood & Ainsworth 2011 (ages 7–11): more learning and about 7× more free-choice play. Replications are mixed. |
| 2 | **Bring a grown-up in** | A "show a grown-up" moment after an activity; a parent page with what was practised and a question to ask. | Moderate. A GraphoGame meta-analysis found near-zero effect overall and about 0.48 only with high adult involvement. A small co-play study (ages 4–5) agrees. |
| 3 | **Story, and characters who ask for things** | One visible request at a time ("I need a B for my bridge"). Story reasons for every task. | Moderate. Fiction is a significant moderator in Sailer & Homner 2020. Sago Mini found pure open play left children asking "what should I do?". |
| 4 | **A companion the child owns and shapes** | A pet or creature the child designs. It speaks to the child, waits, and names what the child actually did. It never sulks or decays. | Moderate. Calvert et al. (ages 3–6): bond with a responsive character improved maths learning. Recurs in the best-regarded products. |
| 5 | **Small, surprising, cosmetic rewards** | Unexpected finds. Avoid "do X, get Y" contracts on things the child already enjoys. | Strong for the effect (Lepper, Greene & Nisbett 1973 in preschoolers; Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999), though Cameron & Pierce dispute the meta-analysis. |
| 6 | **Mostly-succeeding difficulty** | Step up after a run of successes, down after misses. Never three failures in a row. | Weak for children. The "85% rule" (Wilson et al. 2019) comes from simulations and animal data. |
| 7 | **Hints that fade** | First miss: a cue. Second: a demonstration. Third: full support. Remove one rung next time. | Moderate. Shute 2008 review; not child-specific. |
| 8 | **Quiet the screen during learning moments** | During a task show only the target. Save the big celebration for correct learning actions. | Moderate, older learners. Seductive-details meta-analysis (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020); e-book studies at ages 3–5. |
| 9 | **Gentle failure, process praise** | No lives, buzzers, red crosses or game over. "You sounded it out carefully", not "You're so clever". | Moderate. Kamins & Dweck 1999 (ages 5–6). Corpus & Lepper 2007 note the base is narrow. |
| 10 | **Speak everything; size for small hands** | Every instruction voiced with a pulsing target. Targets about 2cm. Controls off the bottom edge. No pinch or flick. | Moderate / practitioner consensus. NN/g, Sesame Workshop 2012, Gelman, Google. |
| 11 | **Choice and personalisation** | The child names the hero and picks cosmetic options. Few choices, never ones that change difficulty. | Moderate. Cordova & Lepper 1996 (elementary age). |
| 12 | **Disguised spaced review** | Bring a new item back after about a minute, later in the session, then the next day, as a normal level. | Moderate in general, weak in early years. |
| 13 | **A collection with visible gaps** | A sticker book or journal with silhouettes for what is not yet found. | Weak. Sago Mini playtests: children carry unfound items as a mental checklist across days. |
| 14 | **Content drops on a rhythm** | A new area or character every few weeks, not everything up front. | Weak. Novelty-decay studies are mostly on students. |
| 15 | **Open play alongside goals** | A home base to decorate, drawing, sandboxes, with a light goal available. | Weak (design inference). |

## 2. Handwriting specifically

| # | Technique | In practice | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | **Fade from demo to unaided** | Animated demo → tap start dots → trace → write with the model hidden. | Weak: product practice (LetterSchool, Writing Wizard), no outcome data. |
| H2 | **Move from tracing to copying early** | After a few traces, copy from a model shown beside the pad. Show varied examples of each letter, not one perfect glyph. | Moderate. Li & James 2016, 72 five-year-olds, novel symbols, short-term only. |
| H3 | **Judge the finished letter, not each stroke** | Keep the child's ink visible; don't gate every stroke behind a verdict. | Moderate. Patchan & Puranik 2016 (preschool, 8 weeks); known from secondhand summaries. |
| H4 | **Reward formation, not scribbling** | Accept any recognisable letter, but full effect only for correct start and stroke order. Tie the effect to the letter shape. | Weak: reviews of products. |
| H5 | **Say the sound while the child writes** | Letter sound or name spoken during the stroke; stroke sounds. | Moderate. Bara & Gentaz; Longcamp et al. 2005. Small studies; one replication found no advantage. |
| H6 | **Short bursts** | Three to five letters, then back to play. | Moderate for ages 5–8 (Graham's 50–100 minutes a week in short sessions). |
| H7 | **Finger or stylus, child's choice** | One preschool study found finger beat stylus. Add palm rejection. | Weak to moderate, younger children. |
| H8 | **Give the parent the dials** | Tolerance, guide visibility, left-handed mode. | Weak: product practice. |

Handwriting by hand beats typing for letter learning in kindergartners (Frontiers in Psychology 2019); the evidence for stylus-on-tablet specifically is unclear.

## 3. Avoid

- Quiz gates that interrupt play.
- "Do X to get Y" reward contracts; leaderboards; visible status items.
- Streak counters, countdowns, loss-framed daily rewards, random loot.
- Characters that plead, cry or guilt the child into staying; companions that decay when the child is away.
- Person praise ("clever girl") and harsh failure feedback.
- Decorative animation and sound during a learning task.
- Text-only instructions or error messages.
- Long unskippable intros or celebrations.
- Endless loops with no natural stopping point.
- Rewarding scribbles or wrong stroke order.

Radesky et al. 2022 found manipulative design in about 80% of 160 apps used by 3–5-year-olds. Its categories: parasocial pressure, fabricated time pressure, navigation constraints, attractive lures, ad pressure. The UK Age Appropriate Design Code asks services to nudge under-13s towards breaks.

## 4. What the well-regarded products do

| Product | What works | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Teach Your Monster to Read | Child makes a monster and takes it on a story journey; reading practice is the progression. | No independent efficacy study found. |
| DragonBox | Rules emerge from manipulating objects; no long explanations. | Transfer to symbolic maths unproven. |
| Khan Academy Kids | Animal guides; child picks a small prize for a collection; adaptive path; drawing for open play. | — |
| Endless Alphabet | No goals; each word acts out its meaning. | Vocabulary more than alphabet. |
| Sago Mini / Toca Boca | No scores or timers; characters open with a request; tiny instant rewards; wordless. | Pure open play left children aimless. |
| GraphoGame | Adaptive letter-sound practice; best trial evidence here. | Small effects without adult involvement. |
| Duolingo ABC | Tap, drag, listen, speak. | Rigid path criticised; efficacy claim unverified. |
| Prodigy Math | RPG with pets and battles. | Maths bolted onto a reward loop; status items and upsell drew a complaint to the FTC from advocacy groups. |

The dividing line: in the praised products learning is the game's verb and rewards are small and cosmetic. In the criticised ones learning is bolted onto a reward loop.

- Never-fail puzzles, gentle respawn, no lives or timers (§1 #9).
- Everything read aloud; 56px+ targets (#10).
- Deep character creator and pets (#11).

- Bug journal and curio cabinet show gaps as silhouettes (#13).
